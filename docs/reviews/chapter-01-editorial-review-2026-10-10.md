## GIS-practitioner reading

The chapter’s useful technical spine was already present: cadastral plans and indexes, triangulation, human computers, census maps, NZMS1 sheets, aerial-photo archives and thematic maps. The reader had to work through repeated abstractions before reaching those concrete details. “Inheritance”, “system”, “infrastructure” and “ingredients” recurred across the opening and close; several paragraphs described what digitisation would eventually do without showing the analogue record or workflow. I replaced those repetitions with specific file types, production tasks, scales, dates and evidence.

## Passages revised

- **Existing map records:** Replaced the stacked opening inventory with a shorter statement of what computer mappers inherited. Kept the institutional context and the maintenance work GIS readers will recognise. Clarified the Tuki map’s purpose and setting, and preserved the National Library’s Huru/Huku spelling difference without inventing a biography. Tightened the Reko–Thomson passage so it does not claim direct copying between particular maps.
- **The parcel record:** Put the plan, observation, title and index relationship in the foreground. Added the 1884–85 report’s counts (1,470 ten-chain sheets and 155 record maps). Replaced the generic “digital problem” language with a practical warning about age, scale and observed accuracy. Kept the distinction between a reproducible line and the historical authority behind it.
- **Coordinates before computers:** Explained how district grids, triangulation and measured baselines fit together. Added the Wairarapa baseline, NZGD49, Rachel Gillanders’ job titles and the 223 control coordinates supplied for the survey vessel Lachlan. Removed the unrelated `entity:traverse-seamless` association from this passage and added the Department of Lands and Survey record.
- **National sheets:** Made the scale and first NZMS1 sheet explicit, then tightened the wartime production description. Replaced the abstract “common backdrop” explanation with the specific limits of a national topographic sheet when it is compared with cadastral and control data.
- **Counting by place:** Added the 674 census plans in 1886, the 895 sub-enumerators in 1901 and the 1945 report’s “population mesh blocks”. Replaced the Fraser “literal demonstration of completeness” punchline with the verifiable detail that he drew his own card at age 45. Connected the paper workflow directly to chapter 16 and Statistics New Zealand’s documented boundary responsibilities.
- **The country from the air:** Kept the overlap, control, stereo measurement and film-index details that matter to practitioners. Removed the unsupported 1959 acquisition date for the Wild A8. Added the sourced scale of the LINZ Crown Aerial Film Library and a link onward to the digital imagery story.
- **Thematic geography:** Replaced the broad list of future GIS themes with the Northland National Resources Survey and the 1959 Descriptive Atlas. Made the key interpretive distinction clear: identical geometry types can represent different evidence and uncertainty.
- **The people behind the sheets:** Replaced a generic statement about hidden labour with a production sequence and named roles. Corrected “more than a million lithographic impressions in a year” to the report’s exact 1884–85 total of 603,341. Kept the women’s work concrete and tied each claim to a linked profile or source.
- **The mapping inheritance:** Removed “institutional ingredients” and condensed the close to the records’ actual formats and the people who maintained their links. The final paragraph now leads into the next chapter’s computer arithmetic.
