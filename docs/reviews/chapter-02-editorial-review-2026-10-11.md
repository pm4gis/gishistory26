# Chapter 2 editorial review — 11 October 2026

## Reader’s view

A working GIS practitioner can follow the chapter’s main distinction: early computers handled survey and engineering arithmetic while most map geometry remained on paper. The original explanation was technically sound in places, but the same point recurred in several sections. Phrases such as “important transitional object”, “the pieces of the puzzle” and “a remarkably human chain” sounded abstract or polished for effect. The ending repeated the paper-map boundary without giving the reader a specific next step.

## Editorial changes

- Rebuilt the opening around an actual workflow: observations in, adjusted coordinates and tables out, with plotting still done on paper.
- Explained the period meaning of “computer” as a job title, using Russell Gladstone Dick’s 1966 account of Department of Lands and Survey staff.
- Kept the ICT 1201 / IBM 650 chronology contested and stated the different strength of the evidence.
- Added the Treasury payroll scale and the practical conditions around the IBM 650 installation.
- Replaced the general Ministry of Works discussion with reported Clutha Valley and Mangaweka route examples. The dollar figures remain identified as period estimates.
- Clarified the difference between NZGD49 (datum) and NZMG (projection), and added a practical warning about historic coordinate transformations.
- Replaced the generic storage/GIS comparison with the checks practitioners make for identifiers, geometry, attributes and feature relationships.
- Reworked the final section to lead into digitising, contouring and photogrammetry in Chapter 3.
- Added chapter links across the paper-map inheritance, computer cartography, remote sensing, land inventory and early GIS stories.
